## Main post (paste-ready, ~2,250 chars)

NSF is putting money behind AI for scientific discovery.

A Dear Colleague Letter — NSF 26-023 — invites proposals under the Genesis Mission, the national effort established by executive order last November. Read what they are asking for: autonomous and semi-autonomous laboratories. AI models combined with novel scientific datasets. Ambitious questions "within reach due to emerging AI capabilities." Workforce and capacity building, with industry cost-share.

This is good, and NSF has a real claim to it. They funded both the fundamental AI research and the domain science this is meant to accelerate.

📌 It is also half the battle — and it is the half we already measure well.

I have spent this month mapping who builds between published evidence and somebody acting on it. The upstream is genuinely instrumented. AI-for-science evaluation now runs 57 agents across more than 2,400 problems on a public leaderboard, with tooling standardised and costs frozen so nobody buys their way up the table.

Every one of those benchmarks grades an artifact the machine produced. Did it find the right papers. Did the code run. Did the reproduction match. Was the summary faithful.

None of them grades whether a person decided better.

That is not a criticism of the benchmarks. It is a description of where the measuring stops — and it stops at the boundary where science leaves its own building.

🔹 74,719 diagnosis codes for what is wrong with you. No agreed metric for whether you are healthy.
🔹 Global surveillance for when medicine harms people — 40M+ reports, 160+ countries. Nothing equivalent for whether it helped.
🔹 A review of 445 language-model benchmarks found 16% used statistical tests, and nearly half define a phenomenon nobody agrees on.

Accelerate discovery into a translation layer nobody can measure and you get more findings, faster, with the same odds of reaching anyone.

👉 The exciting part is real. Autonomous labs will test hypotheses nobody had time for. But the return shows up when an output crosses into a clinic, a guideline, a decision somebody actually makes — and that crossing is the part nobody is funding a benchmark for.

So if you are writing a Genesis proposal: what would your work have to observe, downstream, to know it mattered?

💡 Information can be health. But only if it makes it out of the lab.

## Notes for posting

- **Generous first, then the "and".** Josh's framing: this is exciting and
  important. The post should read as someone who wants this funded, not someone
  waiting to say "but". The turn does not arrive until after NSF has been given
  its due.
- **Do not claim NSF is ignoring translation.** The DCL's second priority is
  foundational research supporting National S&T Challenges, which is broader than
  discovery. The accurate claim is narrower and still holds: every named priority
  sits before the point where a finding reaches a person.
- **No FRESH pitch.** One link, in the comments, to the essay that works the
  argument out. The post has to stand on its own or it reads as bait.
